Example 1: Technology Cluster
User Profile: - Primary Cluster: Technology (score: 6/16, confidence: 38%) - Operational Archetype: Explorer - Primary Reward Driver: Impact - Ecosystem Fit: Solo Sprinter

Generated Narrative (562 words)
You’re drawn to systems, code, and how digital infrastructure works. Your curiosities point toward fields where technology solves real problems — from apps people use daily to the invisible systems that run cities. This is the territory where builders, problem-solvers, and engineers create the digital tools that shape modern life.
If you’re still in high school: Prioritise Computer Science or IT, Mathematics (especially if your school offers Further Maths), and Physics. These build the logical thinking and problem-solving foundation you’ll need. If your school offers it, consider Design Technology or any subject involving systems thinking. Don’t worry if you’re not coding yet — most of the best engineers learned to code outside of class, not in it.
University paths to explore: Computer Science, Software Engineering, Data Science, Artificial Intelligence, Cybersecurity, Information Systems, Computer Engineering (blends hardware and software), or Applied Mathematics with a computing focus. If your university offers it, look for programs that emphasize practical projects over pure theory.
Why these subjects matter: The technology cluster requires three core skills — logical problem-solving (from Maths), understanding how systems interact (from Physics and Computer Science), and the ability to build things that work (from hands-on coding and engineering). These subjects train your brain to think in systems, not just memorize facts.
Where this path can take you: You might start as a Junior Software Engineer at a tech company, a Frontend Developer building user interfaces, a Backend Engineer working on APIs and databases, or a DevOps Engineer managing deployment systems. Early roles often involve writing code, fixing bugs, and learning from senior engineers through code reviews.
As you build experience, paths split into specialization: Senior Engineer leading technical architecture, Tech Lead managing small engineering teams, Site Reliability Engineer keeping systems running at scale, Security Engineer protecting systems from attacks, or Machine Learning Engineer building AI-powered features.
Non-obvious paths: Tech skills open doors beyond traditional software companies. Civic Tech Engineer (building digital government services), Climate Tech Developer (using code to fight environmental problems), HealthTech Engineer (medical software and devices), EdTech Product Engineer (educational platforms), or Legal Tech Developer (modernizing law firms and courts).
How you’ll work: You’re an Explorer — someone who learns by doing and dives deep into problems hands-on. In the technology space, this means you’ll excel in roles that involve troubleshooting, rapid prototyping, and figuring things out as you go rather than following strict documentation. Think startup engineering, site reliability work where you’re debugging production systems at 2am, or technical roles where you’re constantly learning new tools and frameworks. You’ll struggle in environments with excessive process, bureaucracy, or where you’re expected to write detailed design documents before touching code.
What will motivate you: You’re driven by Impact — seeing tangible results from your work. In tech, look for roles where you can see real users benefiting from what you build. Avoid pure backend infrastructure work that feels removed from people. Consider product engineering (features users interact with directly), civic tech (government digital services helping citizens), healthtech (medical software), or developer tools (helping other engineers work better). You’ll lose motivation quickly if your code never ships or if you’re building features nobody uses.
Where you’ll thrive: As a Solo Sprinter, you’ll perform best with autonomy and variety. Remote engineering roles, freelance development, or small teams where you own entire features suit you better than large corporate hierarchies with rigid processes. Look for companies that give engineers freedom to choose their own tools, work async, and measure output by results rather than hours logged. Avoid jobs requiring constant collaboration, back-to-back meetings, or pair programming all day.
The reality check: Technology evolves fast — continuous learning is non-negotiable. The field rewards problem-solving ability over credentials, but burnout rates are high in some roles (early-stage startups, on-call rotations for production systems). Imposter syndrome is common because there’s always someone who knows more. The industry has periods of boom (hiring frenzy) and bust (mass layoffs). Remote work is common but requires serious self-discipline.
This week: Build something. Anything. A simple website, a Python script that automates something annoying, a bot that texts you the weather. It doesn’t matter what — just write code that does something. Use ChatGPT or Claude to help you debug. Don’t take a course. Just build.
This term: Join an online community like r/learnprogramming or find a Discord server for beginners. Start contributing to open source — even fixing typos in documentation counts.
This year: Deploy something to the internet that strangers can use. A tool, a game, a calculator, a meme generator. Put it on your phone’s home screen. That feeling when something you built works for someone else — that’s the job.

Example 2: People/Psychology Cluster
User Profile: - Primary Cluster: People/Psychology (score: 7/16, confidence: 44%) - Operational Archetype: Catalyst - Primary Reward Driver: Recognition - Ecosystem Fit: High-Energy Team Player

Generated Narrative (584 words)
You’re drawn to human behavior, what makes people tick, and how to create conditions where people thrive. Your curiosities point toward fields where understanding psychology, motivation, and interpersonal dynamics is the core skill — not a nice-to-have. This is the territory where therapists, coaches, educators, and organizational designers do their work.
If you’re still in high school: Prioritise Psychology if your school offers it, Biology (essential for understanding the brain and neuroscience paths), English or Literature (strong communication skills matter enormously in this field), and consider Sociology or Philosophy if available. If you’re choosing between sciences, Biology opens more doors than Chemistry or Physics for this cluster.
University paths to explore: Psychology (clinical, cognitive, social, developmental tracks), Counseling, Social Work, Education, Human Resources, Public Health, Organizational Psychology, or Sociology. Some universities offer interdisciplinary programs combining psychology with business, technology (UX research), or public policy — these can be excellent if you’re multi-curious.
Why these subjects matter: Working with people requires understanding both the science (how the brain works, what research says about behavior) and the art (reading emotional cues, building trust, navigating complex social dynamics). Biology gives you the neuroscience foundation. Psychology gives you frameworks for understanding motivation and mental health. English develops the communication clarity essential for therapy, teaching, or coaching.
Where this path can take you: You might start as a Research Assistant in a psychology lab, a Junior HR Coordinator in a company, a Teaching Assistant or Early Career Educator, a Youth Support Worker, or a Trainee Counselor (requires additional supervised hours). Entry-level roles often involve direct service work, case management, or supporting senior professionals.
As you build expertise, paths diverge sharply: Clinical Psychologist (therapy and diagnosis, requires doctorate), School Counselor, HR Business Partner (employee wellbeing and culture), Organizational Development Consultant (helping companies improve team dynamics), Community Mental Health Worker, Educational Psychologist, or Social Worker specializing in child welfare, mental health, or community services.
Non-obvious paths: Psychology and people skills open unexpected doors: UX Researcher (understanding user behavior for tech products), Coaching (executive coaching, life coaching, sports psychology), Conflict Resolution Specialist (mediation, restorative justice), Market Research (understanding consumer behavior), Employee Wellbeing Programs (corporate mental health), or Behavioral Economics (using psychology to shape policy).
How you’ll work: You’re a Catalyst — someone who thrives in fast-moving environments, connecting people and ideas, and seeing the big picture. In people-focused fields, this means you’ll excel in roles that involve facilitation, group dynamics, organizational change, or coaching rather than long-term one-on-one clinical work. Think team coaching, workshop facilitation, HR transformation projects, or community organizing. You’ll struggle in traditional therapy settings that require slow, deep, repetitive one-on-one sessions with the same client for years. Look for dynamic roles where you’re working with multiple people or teams.
What will motivate you: You’re driven by Recognition — being known, respected, and visible for your contributions. In the people/psychology space, this means you’ll thrive when building a public profile: speaking at conferences, writing articles or books, building a coaching practice with a strong personal brand, or leading high-visibility organizational initiatives. You’ll lose motivation in behind-the-scenes support roles where your work isn’t acknowledged. Consider paths where you can build thought leadership: published researcher, keynote speaker on wellbeing topics, Instagram/TikTok educator on mental health, or consultant whose name carries weight.
Where you’ll thrive: As a High-Energy Team Player, you’ll perform best in collaborative, fast-paced environments. Agency work, corporate HR teams, school counseling centers with daily colleague interaction, or community programs where you’re constantly working with others suit you better than solo private practice. Look for roles with lots of meetings, brainstorming sessions, and team projects. Avoid isolated clinical work or remote positions where you’re alone most of the day.
The reality check: Most psychology and counseling careers require a Master’s degree and supervised clinical hours (expensive and time-intensive). Social work and teaching are emotionally demanding with high burnout rates — you’ll absorb other people’s trauma and stress daily. Pay is often lower than tech or business fields despite requiring similar education levels (therapists in private practice: $40-80k; corporate HR: $50-90k; compare to software engineers: $80-150k+). Strong emotional boundaries are essential — you can’t save everyone, and you’ll need therapy yourself to process the work. Managed care and insurance paperwork consume 30-40% of clinical time.
This week: Have three real conversations where you ask someone “What’s actually going on with you?” and then just listen for 10 minutes without offering advice. Notice how hard it is to not interrupt or fix. That’s the job.
This term: Volunteer somewhere people-facing: crisis hotline, tutoring program, elderly care center, youth mentorship. One afternoon a week. See if you can handle the emotional weight.
This year: Read “Maybe You Should Talk to Someone” by Lori Gottlieb (therapist’s memoir) and “The Body Keeps the Score” by Bessel van der Kolk (trauma science). If those books bore you, this field isn’t for you. If they fascinate you, you’re on the right path.

Example 3: Business Cluster
User Profile: - Primary Cluster: Business (score: 5/16, confidence: 31%) - Operational Archetype: Coordinator - Primary Reward Driver: Stability - Ecosystem Fit: Structured Team Player

Generated Narrative (571 words)
You’re drawn to strategy, how organizations work, and what makes markets tick. Your curiosities point toward fields where commerce, profit dynamics, and operational thinking are the core language. This is the territory where managers, strategists, analysts, and entrepreneurs build companies and drive economic value.
If you’re still in high school: Prioritise Mathematics (essential for business analytics and finance), Economics (understanding markets and incentives), and Business Studies if your school offers it. If you’re in a system with A-Levels or equivalent, consider Accounting. Geography can be surprisingly useful for understanding global markets and supply chains. Strong writing skills matter (English/Literature) because business communication is half the job.
University paths to explore: Business Administration, Economics, Finance, Accounting, Marketing, International Business, Supply Chain Management, or Entrepreneurship. Some universities offer specialized tracks like Business Analytics (blends business and data science) or Sustainable Business (for environmentally-conscious paths). If you’re drawn to specific industries, look for business degrees with concentrations: Sports Management, Hospitality Management, Fashion Business, or Healthcare Administration.
Why these subjects matter: Business requires understanding three core systems: how money flows (Finance/Accounting), how markets behave (Economics), and how organizations operate (Management/Operations). Mathematics develops the analytical thinking for financial modeling. Economics teaches you to think in incentives and trade-offs. Business courses teach frameworks for strategy, marketing, and operations. These subjects train you to see organizations as systems with measurable inputs and outputs.
Where this path can take you: You might start as a Business Analyst (analyzing data to improve operations), Junior Marketing Coordinator, Financial Analyst (building models and forecasts), Operations Associate, Sales Development Representative, or Management Trainee in a corporate rotational program. Early roles often involve spreadsheets, presentations, market research, or supporting senior leaders with projects.
As you build experience, paths diverge by function: Marketing Manager (brand strategy, campaigns, growth), Finance Manager (budgets, forecasts, investment decisions), Operations Manager (supply chain, logistics, process optimization), Product Manager (deciding what to build and why), Strategy Consultant (advising companies on big decisions), or Entrepreneur (building your own business, highest risk and reward).
Non-obvious paths: Business skills are portable across industries and contexts: Social Enterprise (for-profit businesses with social missions like TOMS or Warby Parker), Impact Investing (financing companies that do good), Sports Management (running teams or leagues), Startup Operations (the behind-the-scenes work that makes high-growth companies function), Business Development in Tech (partnerships and growth strategy for software companies), or E-commerce Strategy (running online retail operations).
How you’ll work: You’re a Coordinator — someone who excels at planning, organizing, and connecting pieces into functioning systems. In business, this means you’ll thrive in roles that involve project management, program coordination, operations, or cross-functional collaboration. Think program manager running product launches, operations manager optimizing supply chains, or business analyst coordinating between departments. You’ll excel at keeping complex projects on track, managing stakeholders, and ensuring nothing falls through the cracks. Avoid pure creative roles (brand design, content creation) or highly unstructured startup environments where roles are undefined.
What will motivate you: You’re driven by Stability — predictable income, clear career progression, and secure employment. In business, this means corporate roles suit you better than entrepreneurship. Look for established companies (Fortune 500, government-backed enterprises, large regional firms) with structured career ladders, benefits, and low layoff risk. Finance, accounting, and operations roles tend to be more stable than marketing or sales. Avoid startups (high failure rate, volatile pay), commission-based sales (unpredictable income), or consulting (project-based instability). Prioritize companies with strong balance sheets and industries with steady demand (healthcare administration, utilities, government contracting).
Where you’ll thrive: As a Structured Team Player, you’ll perform best in corporate environments with clear processes, defined roles, and collaborative teams. Large companies (banks, consulting firms, multinational corporations), government agencies, or established regional businesses suit you better than chaotic startups or solo entrepreneurship. Look for roles with regular meetings, clear reporting lines, and structured career paths. You’ll excel in matrix organizations where collaboration and stakeholder management are key skills.
The reality check: Business degrees are broad — you need to specialize. Finance pays well but requires strong quantitative skills and long hours (especially investment banking). Marketing is creative but increasingly data-driven and competitive. Entrepreneurship is romanticized; most startups fail, and founders often work 80-hour weeks for years with no salary. Corporate business careers can be political — managing up and navigating office dynamics matters as much as actual performance. Many entry-level business roles involve significant administrative work (spreadsheets, slide decks, emails) before you reach strategic decision-making. The path to senior leadership (VP, C-suite) is narrow and competitive.
This week: Find a local business — a café, a shop, a gym — and observe it for an hour. How many customers? What do they buy? What are the costs? Estimate whether it’s profitable. That’s business thinking.
This term: Read “The Lean Startup” by Eric Ries (even if you never start a business, it teaches how to test ideas) and “Good to Great” by Jim Collins (why some companies win). These are foundational business texts.
This year: Track your own spending for three months. Build a personal budget. Understand where your money goes. If you can’t manage your own finances, you can’t manage a company’s.
