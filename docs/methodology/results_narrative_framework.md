# CORE Assessment Results Narrative Framework
**Version 1.0 | For Content Writers & AI Generation**

---

## Core Principle: Guidance, Not Description

**CRITICAL RULE:** The CORE Assessment provides **GUIDANCE** about possible paths, NOT personality descriptions or labels.

### What This Means:

✅ **DO:** "Your curiosities suggest exploring subjects like..."
✅ **DO:** "Consider career paths where you can..."
✅ **DO:** "Based on your operational style, you'll thrive in environments that..."

❌ **DON'T:** "You are a creative person who..."
❌ **DON'T:** "Your personality type is..."
❌ **DON'T:** "People like you tend to..."

**The user's result is a COMPASS, not a LABEL.** We're showing them directions they can explore, not telling them who they are.

---

## The Results Architecture

Every user receives:

1. **Primary Career Cluster** (1 of 8) — their main "compass direction"
2. **Operational Archetype** (1 of 4) — how they naturally work
3. **Primary Reward Driver** (1 of 5) — what motivates them
4. **Ecosystem Fit** (1 of 4) — where they thrive

The narrative weaves all four together into a **cohesive guidance document** that helps them make decisions about:
- **High school subject choices**
- **University major selection**
- **Career path exploration**
- **Work environment preferences**

---

## Narrative Structure (400-600 words per cluster)

### Section 1: Opening Hook (50-75 words)
**Purpose:** Immediately confirm they're in the right place. Name the territory.

**Format:**
> "You're drawn to [cluster essence]. Your curiosities point toward fields where [what this cluster does]. This is the territory where [specific type of work] happens."

**Example (Technology cluster):**
> "You're drawn to systems, code, and how digital infrastructure works. Your curiosities point toward fields where technology solves real problems — from apps people use daily to the invisible systems that run cities. This is the territory where builders, engineers, and problem-solvers create the digital tools that shape modern life."

### Section 2: The Academic Path (150-200 words)
**Purpose:** Translate their curiosities into concrete school subjects and university majors.

**Format:**
- **High school subjects to prioritize:** List 3-5 specific subjects
- **University majors to explore:** List 5-8 relevant degree programs
- **Why these paths:** Brief explanation of how these subjects develop the skills this cluster requires

**Critical:** Use **regional naming conventions** (e.g., "A-Levels," "Tawjihi," "Maths" not "Math," "Chemistry" not "Sciences").

**Example (Technology cluster):**
> **If you're still in high school:** Prioritise Computer Science or IT, Mathematics (especially if your school offers Further Maths), and Physics. These build the logical thinking and problem-solving foundation you'll need. If your school offers it, consider Design Technology or any subject involving systems thinking.
>
> **University paths to explore:** Computer Science, Software Engineering, Data Science, Artificial Intelligence, Cybersecurity, Information Systems, Computer Engineering (blends hardware and software), or Applied Mathematics with a computing focus.
>
> **Why these subjects matter:** The technology cluster requires three core skills — logical problem-solving (from Maths), understanding how systems interact (from Physics and Computer Science), and the ability to build things that work (from hands-on coding and engineering). These subjects train your brain to think in systems, not just memorise facts.

### Section 3: The Career Landscape (150-200 words)
**Purpose:** Show the breadth of real career paths within this cluster. Make it tangible, not abstract.

**Format:**
- **Entry-level roles** (what they might do in their 20s)
- **Mid-career evolution** (where these paths can lead)
- **Non-obvious paths** (careers people don't immediately associate with this cluster)

**Critical:** Use **real job titles** and **tangible descriptions**, not vague buzzwords.

**Example (Science/Data cluster):**
> **Where this path can take you:**
>
> You might start as a **Research Assistant** in a university lab, a **Data Analyst** at a tech company, a **Clinical Research Coordinator** at a hospital, or a **Junior Scientist** at a biotech firm. Early roles often involve running experiments, analysing datasets, or supporting senior researchers.
>
> As you build expertise, paths split into specialisation: **Senior Data Scientist** designing machine learning models, **Principal Investigator** leading your own research projects, **Epidemiologist** tracking disease patterns, **Genomics Researcher** working on personalised medicine, or **Quantitative Analyst** at a financial firm using data to predict market behaviour.
>
> **Non-obvious paths:** Science and data skills open doors to **Science Policy Advisor** roles (shaping government decisions), **Technical Writer** (translating complex research for the public), **Forensic Scientist** (crime labs, not just TV), or **Environmental Data Analyst** (tracking climate change and sustainability).

### Section 4: Integration with Other Pillars (100-150 words)
**Purpose:** Personalise the guidance by integrating their Operational Archetype, Reward Driver, and Ecosystem Fit.

**Format:** Conditional statements based on their other pillar results.

**Structure:**
```
Your operational style is [archetype] — this means [what this means for their career within this cluster].

You're primarily motivated by [reward driver] — so [how to find this within the cluster].

You thrive in [ecosystem fit] environments — which means [what type of workplace within this cluster suits them].
```

**Example (Technology cluster + Explorer archetype + Impact driver + Solo Sprinter ecosystem):**
> **How you'll work:** You're an **Explorer** — someone who learns by doing and dives deep into problems hands-on. In the technology space, this means you'll excel in roles that involve troubleshooting, building prototypes, and figuring things out as you go rather than following strict documentation. Think startup engineering, site reliability engineering, or technical roles where you're constantly learning new tools.
>
> **What will motivate you:** You're driven by **Impact** — seeing tangible results from your work. In tech, look for roles where you can see real users benefiting from what you build. Avoid pure backend infrastructure work that feels removed from people. Consider product engineering, civic tech, or healthtech — places where code directly helps someone.
>
> **Where you'll thrive:** As a **Solo Sprinter**, you'll perform best with autonomy and variety. Remote roles, freelance engineering, or small teams where you own entire features suit you better than large corporate hierarchies with rigid processes.

### Section 5: The Honest Reality Check (50-75 words)
**Purpose:** Set realistic expectations. Acknowledge the hard parts. Build trust by being honest.

**Format:** A short, grounded paragraph about what's difficult or misunderstood about this path.

**Example (Arts/Media cluster):**
> **The reality check:** Creative fields are competitive and often require years of unpaid or low-paid work before financial stability. If you're motivated by Stability, know that freelance creative work is volatile. Many successful creatives supplement income through teaching, consulting, or hybrid roles. The path requires resilience, self-promotion, and often a side hustle in early years.

### Section 6: Next Steps (50-75 words)
**Purpose:** Give them **one concrete action** they can take this week.

**Format:** 
- One immediate action
- One short-term goal (3-6 months)
- One resource or community to explore

**Example (Engineering cluster):**
> **This week:** Find one physical object in your house (a bicycle, a door hinge, a fan) and try to figure out how it works. Sketch the parts. Google what you don't understand. This is how engineers think.
>
> **This term:** If your school has a maker space, robotics club, or engineering society, show up. If not, find online communities like **r/EngineeringStudents** or YouTube channels like **Real Engineering**.
>
> **This year:** Build something — even something small. A birdhouse. A phone stand. A Raspberry Pi project. Engineering isn't a subject you study; it's a skill you practice.

## The 8 Career Cluster Narratives (Templates)

Below are **content outlines** for each of the 8 clusters. These should be expanded into full 400-600 word narratives following the 6-section structure above.

---

### 1. Technology

**Essence:** Systems, code, digital infrastructure, problem-solving through software

**High school subjects:**
- Computer Science / IT
- Mathematics (+ Further Maths if available)
- Physics
- Design Technology

**University majors:**
- Computer Science
- Software Engineering
- Data Science
- Artificial Intelligence / Machine Learning
- Cybersecurity
- Information Systems
- Computer Engineering
- Applied Mathematics (computational focus)

**Career examples:**
- Software Engineer, Data Engineer, DevOps Engineer, AI/ML Engineer, Cybersecurity Analyst, Systems Architect, Technical Product Manager, Site Reliability Engineer

**Non-obvious paths:**
- Civic Tech (government digital services), Legal Tech, HealthTech, EdTech, Climate Tech, Computational Biology

**Reality check:**
Technology evolves fast — continuous learning is non-negotiable. The field rewards problem-solving ability over credentials, but also comes with high burnout rates in some roles (startups, on-call rotations).

---

### 2. Engineering

**Essence:** Designing, building, and optimizing physical systems and structures

**High school subjects:**
- Physics (essential)
- Mathematics (essential)
- Design Technology / Engineering
- Chemistry (for materials science, chemical engineering)

**University majors:**
- Mechanical Engineering
- Civil Engineering
- Electrical Engineering
- Aerospace Engineering
- Robotics Engineering
- Biomedical Engineering
- Chemical Engineering
- Materials Science

**Career examples:**
- Mechanical Engineer, Civil Engineer, Robotics Engineer, Aerospace Engineer, Design Engineer, Manufacturing Engineer, Quality Assurance Engineer, R&D Engineer

**Non-obvious paths:**
- Patent Law (technical background valued), Engineering Consulting, Technical Sales, Prosthetics Design, Sustainability Engineering, Forensic Engineering

**Reality check:**
Engineering requires years of study and professional certification in most countries. Early-career work often involves lots of CAD modeling, documentation, and testing — not always the glamorous design work you might imagine. Physical prototyping takes time and budget.

### 3. Science/Data

**Essence:** Research, analysis, patterns, understanding how systems work through evidence

**High school subjects:**
- Biology (for life sciences)
- Chemistry
- Physics
- Mathematics (especially Statistics)
- Computer Science (for data science paths)

**University majors:**
- Biology / Life Sciences
- Chemistry
- Physics
- Biochemistry
- Neuroscience
- Data Science / Statistics
- Environmental Science
- Genetics / Genomics

**Career examples:**
- Research Scientist, Data Scientist, Lab Technician, Clinical Research Coordinator, Quantitative Analyst, Epidemiologist, Bioinformatics Specialist, Environmental Scientist

**Non-obvious paths:**
- Science Policy Advisor, Technical Writer (science communication), Forensic Scientist, Pharmaceutical Researcher, Science Journalism, Quantitative Finance

**Reality check:**
Academic research paths require a PhD (7-10 years after high school) and are extremely competitive. Industry data science roles often require a Master's degree. Lab work can be repetitive. Grant writing and funding are constant concerns in research careers.

**High school subjects:**
- Art / Visual Arts
- Design Technology
- Media Studies / Film Studies
- English / Literature (for writing/storytelling)
- Theatre / Drama
- Music (if relevant to your interest)

**University majors:**
- Graphic Design
- Film & Television Production
- Journalism
- Creative Writing
- Fine Arts
- Digital Media
- Animation
- Photography
- Communications

**Career examples:**
- Graphic Designer, Film/Video Editor, Copywriter, Art Director, UX/UI Designer, Photographer, Content Creator, Journalist, Animator, Brand Strategist

**Non-obvious paths:**
- UX Research (blends design and psychology), Medical Illustration, Science Communication, Game Design, Exhibition Curator, Social Media Strategy, Documentary Filmmaking

**Reality check:**
Creative fields are highly competitive. Early-career work is often freelance or contract-based with inconsistent income. Building a portfolio and network is as important as formal education. Many creatives work multiple gigs simultaneously. Rejection is constant — resilience and self-promotion are essential skills.

### 5. Business

**Essence:** Strategy, entrepreneurship, commerce, profit-driven problem-solving, market dynamics

**High school subjects:**
- Mathematics
- Economics
- Business Studies (if available)
- Accounting (if available)
- Geography (for global markets understanding)

**University majors:**
- Business Administration
- Economics
- Finance
- Accounting
- Marketing
- Entrepreneurship
- International Business
- Supply Chain Management

**Career examples:**
- Business Analyst, Marketing Manager, Financial Analyst, Product Manager, Strategy Consultant, Entrepreneur/Founder, Sales Manager, Operations Manager, Investment Banker

**Non-obvious paths:**
- Social Enterprise (for-profit businesses with social missions), Business Development in Tech, Impact Investing, Sports Management, Startup Operations, E-commerce Strategy

**Reality check:**
Business degrees are broad — you need to specialise and build specific skills (finance, marketing, operations, etc.). Entrepreneurship is romanticised; most startups fail, and the path is financially unstable. Corporate business careers can involve long hours, politics, and pressure to hit targets.

### 6. Law/Diplomacy

**Essence:** Governance, negotiation, policy, international relations, systems of rules and justice

**High school subjects:**
- English / Literature (strong writing essential)
- History
- Government & Politics (if available)
- Economics
- Languages (especially for diplomacy)

**University majors:**
- Law (LLB)
- Political Science
- International Relations
- Public Policy
- Criminology
- Philosophy, Politics & Economics (PPE)
- Human Rights

**Career examples:**
- Lawyer (various specialisations), Diplomat, Policy Analyst, Legal Consultant, Paralegal, International Relations Officer, Human Rights Advocate, Political Advisor

**Non-obvious paths:**
- Corporate Compliance, Legal Tech, Mediation/Arbitration, Immigration Law, Sports Law, Environmental Law, Parliamentary Researcher, NGO Policy Director

**Reality check:**
Law requires additional professional qualifications after your degree (bar exams, training contracts). The field is extremely competitive, and entry-level positions (trainee lawyers, paralegals) involve long hours and significant stress. Diplomacy roles often require advanced degrees and language skills. Progress is slow; seniority matters.

### 7. People/Psychology

**Essence:** Human behavior, wellness, community, interpersonal dynamics, mental health, social systems

**High school subjects:**
- Psychology (if available)
- Biology (for neuroscience/clinical paths)
- Sociology
- English / Literature
- Philosophy

**University majors:**
- Psychology
- Counseling / Psychotherapy
- Social Work
- Human Resources
- Education / Teaching
- Public Health
- Sociology

**Career examples:**
- Clinical Psychologist, Counselor/Therapist, Social Worker, HR Manager, Teacher/Educator, Community Outreach Coordinator, UX Researcher, Organisational Development Consultant

**Non-obvious paths:**
- User Experience Research (tech), Sports Psychology, Forensic Psychology, Market Research, Employee Wellbeing Programs, Coaching, Conflict Resolution Specialist

**Reality check:**
Most psychology and counseling careers require a Master's degree and supervised clinical hours (expensive and time-intensive). Social work and teaching are emotionally demanding with high burnout rates. Pay is often lower than tech or business fields despite requiring similar education levels. Strong emotional boundaries are essential.

### 8. Environment

**Essence:** Ecology, sustainability, natural systems, conservation, planetary health

**High school subjects:**
- Biology
- Geography
- Chemistry
- Environmental Science (if available)
- Mathematics (for environmental data)

**University majors:**
- Environmental Science
- Ecology
- Conservation Biology
- Environmental Engineering
- Sustainability Studies
- Marine Biology
- Forestry
- Climate Science

**Career examples:**
- Environmental Consultant, Conservation Scientist, Marine Biologist, Sustainability Coordinator, Environmental Engineer, Wildlife Biologist, Climate Data Analyst, Park Ranger

**Non-obvious paths:**
- Corporate Sustainability (ESG roles), Environmental Law, Green Architecture, Climate Finance, Renewable Energy Engineer, Environmental Journalism, Conservation Policy

**Reality check:**
Environmental careers often require fieldwork (sometimes in remote or uncomfortable conditions). Nonprofit conservation roles tend to pay less than corporate equivalents. The field can be emotionally draining when facing slow policy change or environmental destruction. Academic research paths are highly competitive. Many roles require graduate degrees.

## AI Generation Instructions

When generating a personalized result narrative:

1. **Start with the cluster template above**
2. **Personalize Section 4** based on the user's:
   - Operational Archetype
   - Primary Reward Driver
   - Ecosystem Fit
3. **Adjust Section 5 (Reality Check)** based on reward drivers:
   - If Stability-driven → emphasize income stability/volatility
   - If Impact-driven → emphasize how tangible the impact is
   - If Autonomy-driven → emphasize corporate hierarchy/independence
4. **Write in Kai's voice** (see Voice Guidelines below)

---

## Voice Guidelines: "Kai Tone"

Kai is the brand voice of Tareeq. When writing results:

### DO:
- Use **confident, direct statements**: "This path requires X" not "This path might require X"
- Use **specific, tangible examples**: "Lab work can involve 6-hour pipetting sessions" not "Lab work can be tedious"
- Use **active verbs**: "Build something" not "You could consider building something"
- **Acknowledge hard truths**: Don't sugarcoat. If a path is competitive, say so.
- **Second person ("you")**: "Your curiosities suggest..." not "The user's curiosities..."

### DON'T:
- Use **hedge words**: "possibly," "might," "perhaps," "maybe"
- Use **corporate speak**: "leverage," "synergize," "optimize your potential"
- Use **inspirational clichés**: "follow your dreams," "the sky's the limit," "you can be anything"
- **Over-explain**: Keep it tight. 400-600 words max.

### Tone Examples:

✅ **Good (Kai voice):**
> "Engineering requires years of study and professional certification. Early-career work involves CAD modeling, testing, and documentation — not always the glamorous design work you imagine. But if you're someone who gets satisfaction from seeing a physical thing you designed actually work, this is your territory."

❌ **Bad (too soft/vague):**
> "Engineering might be a good fit if you enjoy problem-solving and are interested in designing things. You could potentially work in many different industries and have a fulfilling career helping people through your innovative creations."

✅ **Good (Kai voice):**
> "This week: Find one broken object and try to fix it. This term: Join your school's robotics club or find r/EngineeringStudents online. This year: Build something, even if it's small."

❌ **Bad (too corporate):**
> "Consider exploring opportunities to engage with engineering-related extracurriculars to enhance your understanding of the field and develop relevant competencies that will optimize your preparedness for future academic pursuits."

## Multi-Curious Special Case

If the user scores as **Multi-Curious** (top 3 clusters within 1 point of each other):

**Narrative Approach:**
- Frame this as a strength: "Your curiosities span multiple domains — you're a connector, not a specialist."
- List all 3 top clusters and explain how they intersect
- Suggest **interdisciplinary paths** that combine clusters:
  - Technology + Business = Product Management, Tech Entrepreneurship
  - Science + Arts = Science Communication, Medical Illustration
  - Environment + Law = Environmental Policy, Conservation Law
  - People + Technology = UX Research, EdTech
  - Engineering + Arts = Industrial Design, Game Design
  - Business + Law = Corporate Law, Compliance
  - Science + People = Public Health, Community Science
  - Environment + Business = Sustainability Consulting, Impact Investing

**Example opening:**
> "You're multi-curious — your interests span Technology, Science, and Business. This isn't confusion; it's a different kind of clarity. You're drawn to intersections, not silos. The modern world rewards people who can speak multiple languages — not just human languages, but the language of code, the language of research, and the language of markets."

## Final Checklist for Each Result Narrative

Before publishing any result narrative, ensure:

- [ ] It's 400-600 words (not longer)
- [ ] It uses regional subject names (A-Levels, Tawjihi, etc.)
- [ ] It lists **specific, real job titles**
- [ ] It includes a reality check (acknowledges hard parts)
- [ ] It gives **one concrete next step**
- [ ] It's written in Kai voice (direct, confident, no fluff)
- [ ] It integrates operational archetype, reward driver, ecosystem fit
- [ ] It focuses on **guidance** (what to explore) not **description** (who you are)
- [ ] It avoids personality labels ("You're creative," "You're analytical")
- [ ] It passes the "Would a 17-year-old find this useful?" test
